About Cystic Fibrosis

Cystic Fibrosis is a genetic disease that affects roughly 165 000 people across 94 countries. It causes the patient to have abnormally thick mucus which can damage internal organs.

Genetics

Cystic Fibrosis is a mutation in the CFTR(Cystic Fibrosis Transmembrane Conductance Regulator) which is located in chromosome 7. It's a recessive gene so the person must inherit 2 mutated copies in order to have Cystic Fibrosis.

The gene controls how salt and water move in and out of cells. When it doesn’t work properly, the body makes thick, sticky mucus that affects the lungs, pancreas, and digestive system.

Diagnosis

The main method for diagnosing Cystic Fibrosis is the Sweat test. Other methods include prenatal testing (testing during pregnancy), Fecal Elastase Test(testing the stool for an enzyme made by the pancreas), and Newborn Screening(A sample of blood is taken from the baby's heel and tested for immunoreactive trypsinogen) The Nasal Potential Difference Test is also used when other tests do not provide a clear answer. This test measures how salt moves across cells inside the nose.

Symptoms

CF symptoms can vary, depending on which organs are affected and how severe the condition is. The patient can experience infertility, pancreatitis (inflammation of the pancreas), salty skin, lung and sinus infections, severe constipation, wheezing, foul smelling poo, and getting tired easily. The symptoms can also get better or worse at different times.

Treatment

Unfortunately, there are no existing cures for Cystic Fibrosis. However, there are treatments that can make the symptoms feel better such as antibiotics, mucus thinners, and CFTR modulators.

CFTR modulator therapies are designed to correct the malfunctioning protein made by the mutated CFTR gene. Since different mutations cause different defects in the protein, the medications that have been developed so far are effective only in people with specific mutations.
